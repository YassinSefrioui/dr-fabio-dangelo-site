Metatarsalgia: pain under the ball of the foot

Metatarsalgia is not a disease but a symptom: pain under the ball of the foot. Treating it well means identifying what is producing it.

What is metatarsalgia?

Metatarsalgia is pain localised under the ball of the foot, where the metatarsal heads take load. It appears when one or more of those heads carries more pressure than it should — because of a deformity, an imbalance in metatarsal length, or an alteration in gait mechanics.

The term therefore describes a symptom rather than a diagnosis. Effective treatment is not aimed at the pain but at whatever is concentrating load at that point.

Symptoms

  • Pain under the ball of the foot, worse when walking, running or standing.
  • The feeling of walking on a stone or of a rucked-up sock.
  • Calluses and hard skin under the metatarsal heads.
  • Pain that increases barefoot on hard floors or in thin, flat shoes.
  • Burning, tingling or radiation into the toes.
  • Relief on resting and elevating the foot.

Causes and risk factors

  • Hallux valgus: as the big toe loses its load-bearing role, weight transfers to the central metatarsals.
  • Hammer toes, which pull the plantar fat pad forwards and leave the metatarsal head unprotected.
  • Metatarsals of unequal length or excessively plantarflexed.
  • Cavus foot, with a high arch and forefoot overload.
  • Age-related atrophy of the plantar fat pad.
  • Impact activities, high heels or excessively thin soles.
  • Transfer metatarsalgia following previous forefoot surgery.

How is it diagnosed?

Because metatarsalgia is a symptom, the purpose of the consultation is to identify what is producing it. Examination localises the exact point of pain, assesses movement at the metatarsophalangeal joints, reads the pattern of calluses — which mark where pressure is concentrating — and analyses gait.

The weight-bearing X-ray is the key investigation: it shows the metatarsal formula, meaning the relative length of each metatarsal, and reveals associated deformities such as hallux valgus or hammer toes. Pressure-plate analysis quantifies load distribution, while ultrasound or MRI help exclude a neuroma, a stress fracture or plantar plate inflammation.

Non-surgical treatment

Conservative treatment aims to redistribute forefoot pressure. The main tool is a custom orthotic with a metatarsal dome, which offloads the painful metatarsal heads. It is combined with well-cushioned footwear with adequate toe-box room, podiatric treatment of the calluses — which are a consequence, not a cause — physiotherapy, and a temporary adjustment to impact activities.

For many patients these measures are enough. When pain persists, it usually signals a structural deformity that the orthotic can compensate for but not correct.

When is surgery indicated?

Surgery is considered when pain persists despite orthotics and properly delivered conservative treatment, when calluses systematically reappear, when a structural deformity is concentrating load, or when pain limits work or sport.

Metatarsalgia surgery is rarely undertaken in isolation: it is almost always part of a plan to rebalance the forefoot.

Dr. D’Angelo’s technique

The surgical objective is to redistribute pressure and restore normal forefoot biomechanics, not simply to remove the painful area. Depending on the underlying cause, treatment may include minimally invasive osteotomies to reposition the overloaded metatarsals, soft-tissue balancing, or correction of the associated deformities generating the load transfer.

Guided by the SPARE™ Method, Dr. Fabio D’Angelo plans these procedures percutaneously or minimally invasively wherever clinically appropriate, through very small incisions and without bulky fixation hardware. Where metatarsalgia coexists with hallux valgus, correcting the first ray is usually the most decisive part of treatment. All options are set out under foot and ankle surgical procedures.

Recovery

Recovery depends on the extent of the correction. With minimally invasive procedures early weight-bearing in a post-operative shoe is generally permitted, and forefoot swelling settles progressively over several weeks.

Where osteotomies have been performed it is important to respect bone healing times and to return to impact in stages. Dr. D’Angelo will set out the timeline for the procedure carried out.

Frequently asked questions

Why does my forefoot hurt when I walk barefoot?

Without the cushioning of a shoe, load falls directly on the metatarsal heads. If one of them carries more pressure than it should, a hard floor makes the symptom immediately obvious.

Do orthotics cure metatarsalgia?

Orthotics redistribute pressure and control pain well in many patients, but they compensate for the cause without correcting it. Where there is an underlying structural deformity, relief lasts as long as the orthotic is worn.

I had foot surgery and now my forefoot hurts. Is that normal?

It may be transfer metatarsalgia: when a previous correction alters load distribution, a neighbouring metatarsal takes more pressure. This is one of the situations Dr. D’Angelo regularly assesses in revision surgery.
