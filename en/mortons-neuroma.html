Morton’s neuroma: diagnosis and treatment in Barcelona

Burning pain between the toes, tingling, and the sensation of walking on a pebble or a folded sock are the classic description of Morton’s neuroma.

What is Morton’s neuroma?

Morton’s neuroma is a fibrous thickening of the interdigital nerve running between the metatarsal heads, most often in the third space, between the third and fourth toes. It is not a tumour: it is the nerve’s response to repeated mechanical compression against the intermetatarsal ligament.

That thickening makes the nerve still more vulnerable to compression, so the problem tends to perpetuate itself and symptoms become progressively more intense and more frequent.

Symptoms

  • Burning or electric pain in the ball of the foot, radiating into the toes.
  • The sensation of standing on a pebble, a marble or a folded sock.
  • Tingling, numbness or a dead feeling in the affected toes.
  • Worse in narrow or high-heeled shoes and when walking.
  • Characteristic relief on taking the shoe off and massaging the forefoot.
  • No external signs: the foot usually looks entirely normal.

Causes and risk factors

  • Narrow or high-heeled footwear, compressing the forefoot and shifting load forwards.
  • A wide forefoot or an imbalance in metatarsal length.
  • Metatarsalgia and chronic forefoot overload.
  • Flat foot or cavus foot, both of which alter pressure distribution.
  • Repeated impact on the forefoot, as in running or dancing.
  • Other forefoot deformities such as hallux valgus or hammer toes.

How is it diagnosed?

Diagnosing Morton’s neuroma is primarily clinical. Examination reproduces the pain by squeezing the forefoot from side to side while pressing on the affected intermetatarsal space; a characteristic click is often felt, known as Mulder’s sign.

Imaging confirms the suspicion and, above all, rules out other causes of forefoot pain: ultrasound measures the thickening of the nerve in real time, and MRI is useful in doubtful cases or where more than one neuroma is suspected. A weight-bearing X-ray does not show the neuroma itself, but it does show the metatarsal formula and any associated deformities that may be generating the compression.

Non-surgical treatment

Morton’s neuroma responds well to conservative treatment in a significant proportion of cases, particularly when started early. The basis is decompressing the forefoot: wide-lasted, low-heeled footwear, and orthotics with a metatarsal dome or bar that separates the metatarsal heads and offloads the affected space.

Injections, physiotherapy and a temporary change to impact activities can be added. The aim is to break the cycle of compression and inflammation before the thickening becomes established.

When is surgery indicated?

Surgery is considered when pain persists despite properly delivered conservative treatment, when injections stop working or their effect lasts less and less, and when symptoms limit walking, work or sport.

Before surgery is indicated it is essential to confirm the diagnosis and rule out other causes of forefoot pain, since several conditions share similar symptoms.

Dr. D’Angelo’s technique

Dr. Fabio D’Angelo uses minimally invasive and endoscopic techniques wherever clinically appropriate, consistent with the SPARE™ Method. The approach prioritises decompressing the nerve and correcting the mechanical cause compressing it, while preserving the surrounding tissues.

Where the neuroma is the consequence of forefoot overload or an associated deformity, treating the nerve alone leaves the underlying problem untouched. Planning therefore assesses the forefoot as a whole and may include simultaneous correction of a metatarsalgia or a hallux valgus. The full range of options is set out under foot and ankle surgical procedures.

Recovery

Recovery after Morton’s neuroma surgery is usually quick and generally allows early weight-bearing in a post-operative shoe, according to your personalised plan. The characteristic burning pain improves markedly within a short time.

It is worth knowing that if treatment includes resecting the nerve, a permanently numb area remains between the affected toes. It is neither painful nor limiting, but it is a definitive change in sensation and is always explained before the operation.

Frequently asked questions

Can Morton’s neuroma be cured without surgery?

In a significant proportion of cases, yes, especially when treated early: wide footwear, orthotics with a metatarsal dome and injections control symptoms in many patients. Surgery is reserved for pain that persists despite properly delivered conservative treatment.

Why does it only hurt in certain shoes?

Because the mechanism is compression. A narrow shoe squeezes the metatarsal heads together and traps the nerve, and a high heel shifts load onto the forefoot. Taking the shoe off releases the compression, which is why the pain eases immediately.

Will my foot be numb after surgery?

If the procedure includes resecting the affected nerve, a permanently numb area remains between the corresponding toes. It is not painful and does not affect walking, and most patients clearly prefer it to the pain they had before.
