Bunionette (tailor’s bunion): treatment in Barcelona

The bunionette is the bunion’s counterpart on the outer border of the foot: a painful prominence at the base of the little toe that rubs against footwear and widens the forefoot.

What is a bunionette?

A bunionette, or tailor’s bunion, is a painful prominence on the outer side of the foot, at the head of the fifth metatarsal. It develops when that metatarsal drifts outwards while the little toe angles inwards — a mirror image of the mechanism behind a classic bunion.

The name comes from the traditional posture of tailors, who worked cross-legged with the outer border of the foot pressed against the floor. We now know the cause is primarily anatomical and biomechanical rather than postural.

Symptoms

  • A painful prominence on the outer border of the foot, level with the little toe.
  • Irritation, redness and bursitis from rubbing against the shoe.
  • Callus on the outer aspect or under the fifth metatarsal head.
  • Visible widening of the forefoot.
  • Difficulty wearing closed, sports or narrow-lasted shoes.
  • Drift of the little toe towards the fourth toe.

Causes and risk factors

  • Inherited predisposition in the shape of the fifth metatarsal.
  • A constitutionally wide forefoot.
  • Narrow or pointed footwear, which aggravates both rubbing and deformity.
  • Flat foot or pronation, altering load distribution across the forefoot.
  • Coexistence with a bunion (hallux valgus), which is very common: when both are present the forefoot widens from both sides.
  • Inflammatory arthritis.

How is it diagnosed?

Diagnosis is clinical and confirmed with a weight-bearing X-ray, which allows the angle between the fourth and fifth metatarsals to be measured and the type of bunionette classified: a prominent metatarsal head, excessive curvature of the fifth metatarsal, or an increased splay away from the fourth.

That distinction is not academic: it determines which type of correction is required. The X-ray also shows whether a hallux valgus coexists and what the true width of the forefoot is — both essential for planning a complete correction.

Non-surgical treatment

As with a classic bunion, conservative measures relieve symptoms but do not correct the bony deviation. The first step is moving to a wide last in a flexible material that does not compress the outer border of the foot. To that can be added silicone protection to reduce rubbing, orthotics to redistribute forefoot load, podiatric treatment of the callus, and anti-inflammatories during periods of greater irritation.

When is surgery indicated?

Surgical correction is considered when pain persists despite a change of footwear and conservative treatment, when bursitis or callus keeps recurring, or when the widened forefoot makes normal shoes unwearable.

The bunionette is frequently corrected in the same operation as the bunion on the first toe. Treating both deformities together is what makes genuine foot width reduction surgery possible, narrowing the forefoot as a whole and markedly improving footwear options.

Dr. D’Angelo’s technique

Guided by the principles of the SPARE™ Method, Dr. Fabio D’Angelo treats the bunionette using endoscopic and minimally invasive techniques wherever clinically appropriate, through millimetre incisions and without needing to open the joint.

The plan is designed individually to realign the fifth metatarsal while preserving healthy anatomy, minimise soft-tissue trauma and reduce the width of the forefoot. Where it coexists with hallux valgus, planning is done jointly so the whole forefoot is balanced in a single operation. The detail is set out under foot and ankle surgical procedures.

Recovery

Because this is usually a minimally invasive procedure, most patients can bear weight in a post-operative shoe within the first few days, following the plan they are given. Swelling along the outer border of the foot takes several weeks to settle and governs when normal footwear becomes comfortable again.

When the correction is combined with that of the first toe, recovery follows the same timeline as bunion surgery without extending it significantly.

Frequently asked questions

Is a bunionette the same as a bunion?

It is the same mechanism on the opposite side of the foot. A bunion affects the first metatarsal and the big toe; a bunionette affects the fifth metatarsal and the little toe. Many patients have both, and in that case the forefoot widens from both sides.

Can a bunion and a bunionette be corrected in the same operation?

Yes, and that is the usual approach when both are symptomatic. Correcting the two deformities in the same procedure reduces the overall width of the forefoot and avoids going through two separate recoveries.

Will I be able to wear narrow shoes again?

The aim is to give you a healthy, pain-free foot so you have more footwear options, not fewer. Comfort in narrow lasts depends on each patient’s anatomy and on the degree of correction achieved.
