Brachymetatarsia: correcting a short metatarsal

Brachymetatarsia is an uncommon condition in which one or more metatarsals are abnormally short. It is a functional and an aesthetic problem at once, and Dr. D’Angelo treats it regularly.

What is brachymetatarsia?

Brachymetatarsia is a congenital condition in which one or more metatarsals stop growing early because the growth plate closes prematurely. The result is an abnormally short metatarsal and, with it, a toe visibly shorter than the rest, often riding above its neighbours.

It most often affects the fourth metatarsal and is more common in women. It may involve one foot or both, and sometimes more than one metatarsal.

It usually becomes obvious at the end of growth, in adolescence, when the rest of the foot finishes developing and the difference in length becomes plain.

Symptoms and impact

  • A toe visibly shorter than those beside it, frequently elevated.
  • Pain under the forefoot from overload of the neighbouring metatarsals (metatarsalgia).
  • Calluses under the adjacent metatarsal heads, which take on the short toe’s share of load.
  • Rubbing of the elevated toe against footwear.
  • Difficulty finding comfortable shoes.
  • Discomfort about being barefoot in public, with a real impact on quality of life.

The aesthetic dimension of brachymetatarsia is not a minor matter. Many patients present after years of avoiding the beach, the pool or open footwear, and that limitation is a legitimate part of the case for treatment.

Causes

  • Idiopathic premature closure of the metatarsal growth plate, the most common cause.
  • A hereditary component, with a family history in many cases.
  • Association with syndromes such as Down syndrome or pseudohypoparathyroidism.
  • Sequel of childhood trauma, infection or surgery damaging the physis.

Non-surgical treatment

There is no conservative treatment that lengthens the metatarsal. Non-surgical measures are limited to easing the consequences: custom orthotics that redistribute load towards the short metatarsal and offload the overworked neighbours, silicone protection under the elevated toe, footwear with a deep toe box to prevent rubbing, and podiatric treatment of the calluses.

These measures can control pain well, but they change neither the length of the toe nor its appearance.

When is surgery indicated?

Surgery is considered when there is pain from forefoot overload, when calluses under the neighbouring metatarsals are persistent, when the elevated toe rubs continually against footwear, or when the appearance of the foot is shaping the patient’s daily life.

Correction is normally carried out once growth is complete.

Dr. D’Angelo’s technique

The objective is to restore normal metatarsal length, recovering the metatarsal formula, improving load distribution across the forefoot and returning a harmonious appearance to the foot. Depending on the case, there are two broad routes.

Gradual lengthening by distraction osteogenesis uses an external fixator to separate the bone ends by a fraction of a millimetre each day, allowing new bone to form in the space created. It achieves greater lengthening, at the cost of a prolonged treatment period wearing the fixator.

Single-stage lengthening interposes a bone graft and stabilises the metatarsal immediately. It avoids the external fixator and shortens the process, though the length that can be gained is more limited and depends on the tolerance of the soft tissues, which are the real limiting factor in this surgery.

Dr. Fabio D’Angelo selects the appropriate route according to the length to be recovered, the number of metatarsals involved and each patient’s goals, applying the principles of the SPARE™ Method. More information under foot and ankle surgical procedures.

Recovery

Brachymetatarsia demands more patience than the rest of forefoot surgery, because what is being asked of the body is to form new bone. In gradual lengthening, the distraction period is followed by the time needed for the bone to consolidate before the fixator is removed; in single-stage lengthening, the graft has to integrate.

In both cases weight-bearing is permitted progressively, and physiotherapy matters for maintaining movement in the toe. The results, both functional and aesthetic, are seen definitively once consolidation is complete.

Frequently asked questions

How much can a short toe be lengthened?

It depends on the case and, above all, on the soft tissues: skin, tendons and the neurovascular bundle are the limiting factor, not the bone. Gradual lengthening allows greater gains than single-stage lengthening. The realistic figure for your case is determined at the consultation.

Is this purely cosmetic surgery?

No. Brachymetatarsia alters load distribution across the forefoot and causes pain and calluses under the neighbouring metatarsals, which take on the short toe’s function. The aesthetic improvement is real and legitimate, but the indication is functional as well.

At what age can it be operated on?

Correction is normally carried out once growth is complete, to avoid interfering with growth plates that are still active. The initial assessment can be made earlier.
