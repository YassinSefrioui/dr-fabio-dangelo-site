Percutaneous or endoscopic: how they differ

"Minimally invasive", "percutaneous" and "endoscopic" are often used interchangeably. They are not the same, and the difference matters when you are trying to understand what is being proposed.

Last reviewed: August 2026

Minimally invasive: the umbrella term

Minimally invasive surgery describes any technique that achieves the surgical goal through small incisions while limiting damage to the soft tissues. It is a category, not a specific procedure.

Several very different approaches sit inside it, which is where the other two terms come in.

Percutaneous: working through the skin

In percutaneous surgery the surgeon works through incisions a few millimetres across, using dedicated burrs and micromotors, and is guided by intraoperative fluoroscopy: a live X-ray image showing the position of the instruments and the bone.

It is a mature and highly effective technique. Its limitation is that the surgeon does not see directly what they are doing: they interpret an X-ray image, which shows bone very well and soft tissue not at all.

Endoscopic: working under direct vision

In endoscopic surgery a camera is introduced through one of the incisions. The surgeon sees the inside of the joint and the intermetatarsal space on a screen, live and magnified.

The practical difference is that the soft tissues are visible: the capsule, the ligaments, the contracted structures limiting the correction. They can be treated selectively rather than inferred.

It is also a technically more demanding and less widely available approach, which is why it is not offered everywhere.

How the choice is made

They are not rivals: they are combined. The surgical plan is built from the examination and the weight-bearing X-rays, and frequently includes both percutaneous and endoscopic steps in the same operation.

Broadly, bone work lends itself to a percutaneous approach, while soft-tissue work and checking the correction benefit from endoscopic vision. Endoscopic TightRope® correction is a particular case: it avoids the bone cut altogether and relies almost entirely on endoscopic control.

What determines the choice is the deformity, not a preference for a technology. That is the principle behind the SPARE™ Method.

What to ask at the consultation

  • Will bone be cut in my case, and why?
  • Which part of the procedure is percutaneous and which is endoscopic?
  • Will fixation hardware be used? Would it need removing?
  • Will other forefoot deformities be corrected at the same time?
  • What recovery timeline does the proposed plan imply?

Frequently asked questions

Does percutaneous surgery leave a scar?

The incisions are a few millimetres across and the resulting marks are usually very discreet, though like any scar they depend on the individual patient’s skin.

Is one safer than the other?

Both are established techniques in the hands of a surgeon experienced in them. What matters is not the label but whether the approach suits the specific deformity.

Can I request a particular technique?

You can and should raise your preferences and your questions. The final indication, though, is set by what the examination and the weight-bearing X-rays show.
