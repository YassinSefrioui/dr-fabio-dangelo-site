Why bunions come back after surgery

No honest surgeon can guarantee a bunion will never come back. It is worth understanding why it happens, because nearly all the factors can be addressed.

Last reviewed: August 2026

Recurrence is real and worth saying out loud

Recurrent hallux valgus is a recognised reality in the foot surgery literature. It is neither an exceptional failure nor a rarity: it is a known complication every forefoot surgeon lives with.

Saying so openly is not pessimism. It is the only way to have a useful conversation about reducing the risk.

Under-correction of the deformity

The most common cause. A bunion is not just a bump on the side of the foot: it is a deviation of the first metatarsal, often with a rotational component and with instability at the base of the first ray.

If surgery corrects the visible part but not the full deviation of the metatarsal, the deformity keeps the tendency that produced it. The early result can look good and deteriorate over the following months.

That is why planning is done on weight-bearing X-rays rather than on how the foot looks: the real angles have to be measured before deciding.

Instability of the first ray

In some feet the joint between the first metatarsal and the cuneiform is hypermobile. The metatarsal can be realigned perfectly, but if its base remains unstable it will tend to drift again.

Detecting that hypermobility before operating changes the surgical plan. It is one of the reasons clinical examination remains irreplaceable.

What is not down to the surgeon

  • The anatomical and family predisposition that caused the bunion does not disappear with surgery.
  • Generalised ligamentous laxity makes recurrence more likely.
  • Inflammatory conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis keep acting on the joint.
  • Going back long-term to narrow, high-heeled footwear reintroduces the deforming force.
  • Not following post-operative instructions through the healing period.

How the risk is reduced

With planning based on measurements rather than impressions; by correcting the whole deformity and not only its visible part; by treating the associated deformities — hammer toes, metatarsalgia — that alter load distribution; and by respecting the healing period.

The SPARE™ Method adds one more element: preserving healthy anatomy leaves more options available if something ever does need doing again.

If the bunion has already come back

Recurrence is not the end of the road. Dr. D’Angelo regularly assesses cases of recurrent deformity, persistent pain, stiffness or problems with hardware from a previous operation.

The assessment starts from scratch: full examination, weight-bearing X-rays and, where needed, further imaging to understand what failed. We have described the process in what revision surgery involves.

Frequently asked questions

Can the bunion come back?

No surgical procedure can guarantee a bunion will never recur. However, careful planning, precise correction and following the post-operative instructions significantly reduce the risk.

How long does recurrence take to appear?

It varies enormously. An under-corrected deformity can deteriorate within the first months, while recurrence driven by anatomical predisposition may take years to become symptomatic.

Do heels cause recurrence?

Narrow, high-heeled footwear does not cause a bunion on its own, but it does reintroduce the force pushing the big toe outwards. Occasional wear is a different matter from daily, prolonged wear.
