The endoscopic TightRope® technique, explained

Most bunion operations cut the bone. This one does not. Here is what makes endoscopic TightRope® correction different, and who it can help.

Last reviewed: August 2026

The problem with osteotomy

For decades, correcting a bunion has meant much the same thing: cut the first metatarsal, move it into the right position and fix it with screws while it heals. That is an osteotomy, and it works.

But it has a cost. Cutting bone means a healing period during which the correction depends on the fixation; it means hardware that sometimes has to come out; and it means solving an alignment problem through a permanent change to the anatomy.

Dr. Fabio D’Angelo asked a different question: if the deformity exists because the first metatarsal has drifted away from the second, why not pull it back rather than cut it?

What the TightRope® system is

The TightRope® is a high-strength suture system with two small metal buttons at its ends. It is passed through the first and second metatarsals and, when tensioned, draws the two bones together and restores the anatomical relationship between them.

The correction is not held by a screw inside a cut bone but by a suture band working the way a ligament would: holding the first metatarsal in position while the soft tissues adapt to the new alignment.

The system comes from ankle surgery, where it has been used for years to stabilise the syndesmosis. Its application to hallux valgus is more recent.

What the endoscopic approach adds

This is the difference Dr. D’Angelo has developed. Placing a TightRope® in the forefoot through an open approach requires considerable exposure — precisely what you were trying to avoid.

Working endoscopically — with a camera that shows the inside of the joint and the intermetatarsal space through millimetre incisions — it becomes possible to release the contracted structures, place the implant and check the correction directly, without opening the foot.

That direct view is what widens the indications. The usual objection to the TightRope® is that it only suits mild or moderate deformities; Dr. D’Angelo’s endoscopic experience lets him treat a broader spectrum than is normally considered suitable, because he can address the soft tissues limiting the correction rather than working blind.

What it means for the patient

  • The bone is not cut, so there is no wait for an osteotomy to heal.
  • Millimetre incisions instead of an open approach.
  • Healthy anatomy is preserved, in line with the SPARE™ Method.
  • Less soft-tissue trauma, which drives much of the post-operative swelling.
  • The correction can be combined with treating other forefoot deformities at the same time.

What does not change: forefoot swelling takes months to settle whatever the technique, and the timeline for normal footwear and sport follows that. The detail is in recovery week by week.

Is it right for everyone?

No. No technique is, and that is precisely the premise of the SPARE™ Method: choose the approach that suits the foot rather than applying one approach to every foot.

Joint stiffness, advanced arthritis of the first metatarsophalangeal joint, deformities with a marked rotational component or compromised bone quality may point towards a different plan, or a combination of techniques. That decision is made in clinic, with the examination and weight-bearing X-rays in front of you.

If you want to compare the available approaches, we have written about the difference between percutaneous and endoscopic correction.

Frequently asked questions

Can you feel the implant inside the foot?

Most patients cannot. The buttons have a very low profile and sit against the cortex of the bone, beneath the soft tissues.

Does the hardware have to be removed later?

Unlike the screws used in an osteotomy, the system is not intended to be removed. That would only be considered if it caused problems, which is uncommon.

Is this an experimental technique?

The TightRope® system has been in clinical use for years, initially in ankle surgery. What is specific to this practice is the fully endoscopic approach to placing it in the forefoot.
