Bunion surgery recovery, week by week

The question that comes up most in clinic is not whether the surgery works, but how long it takes before life goes back to normal. Here is the usual pattern.

Last reviewed: August 2026

First, why the timeline is a guide

No foot operation runs to a fixed calendar. The severity of the deformity, the technique used, whether one foot or both were operated on, your job and your own healing rate all move the dates.

What follows describes the usual pattern after a minimally invasive or endoscopic correction in a patient without complications. Your personalised plan is the one that counts.

The first few days

The aim of this phase is controlling swelling, and it is achieved mostly through rest and elevation: the foot above heart level for most of the day. It is the single measure that most influences how everything else goes.

You will have a specific dressing — which must not be disturbed, because it forms part of the correction — and a stiff-soled post-operative shoe. With minimally invasive techniques, many patients can bear weight from the start, as directed.

Discomfort peaks in the first days and is managed with the prescribed medication, local cold and elevation.

The first weeks

Swelling starts to settle and things begin to feel better. This is also the point of greatest risk of doing too much: the foot hurts less, but inside it is still healing.

The post-operative shoe stays on, and dressings and reviews follow the schedule you are given. Walking short, functional distances is fine; walking for exercise is not yet.

Driving depends on which foot was operated on, the type of surgery, and whether you can brake safely. If it was the right foot, it usually takes longer. It is a clinical decision, not a date in the diary.

Back into shoes and back to work

The move to normal footwear comes when swelling allows, and it is rarely abrupt: a wide, flexible, comfortably soled shoe first, and only later the rest of the wardrobe.

Patients with desk-based jobs generally return well before those who spend the day standing, walk a lot or do physical work. If you can work from home with the foot elevated, the return is earlier.

Forefoot swelling at the end of the day is normal for a good while and does not mean something is wrong.

Returning to sport

The return is staged and always follows the same order: normal walking first, then low-impact exercise — stationary bike, swimming, cross-trainer — and impact activity last.

Running is the last thing to come back, and it is worth not forcing it: the forefoot absorbs several times body weight with every stride. We have given it a piece of its own, returning to running after forefoot surgery.

What is normal and what is not

  • Normal: swelling that fluctuates for months, particularly at the end of the day and in warm weather.
  • Normal: some stiffness in the big toe that improves gradually with the prescribed mobilisation.
  • Normal: patches of reduced sensation around the incisions.
  • Get in touch: pain that increases rather than decreases, fever, spreading redness or discharge.
  • Get in touch: calf pain or asymmetrical swelling of the leg.

When in doubt, one call too many beats one too few.

Frequently asked questions

When can I walk?

Walking is encouraged in line with your personalised recovery plan. Many patients can bear weight in a protective post-operative shoe soon after surgery, though the timeline varies with the severity of the bunion and the procedure performed.

How long does the swelling last?

Forefoot swelling is the most persistent symptom of the whole recovery and can fluctuate for several months, especially at the end of the day. It does not indicate a problem.

Will I be able to wear heels again?

Many patients can wear them again for special occasions once healing is complete, though comfort depends on the height and style of the shoe as well as on your own anatomy.
