Returning to running after forefoot surgery

Running is the last activity to come back after forefoot surgery, and the one most often rushed. These are the variables that actually set the timeline.

Last reviewed: August 2026

Why running comes last

Walking loads the forefoot with roughly body weight. Running loads it with several times that, concentrated into a shorter contact and a smaller area — right where the surgery was done.

The push-off phase also demands considerable extension of the first metatarsophalangeal joint, which is precisely the joint likely to be stiff during recovery.

It is not that running is dangerous, but that it requires healing to be complete and movement to be restored. Before that, no amount of hurry pays off.

What sets the timeline

  • Whether an osteotomy was performed: cut bone has its own healing schedule.
  • How many rays were operated on, and whether associated deformities were corrected.
  • The movement recovered in the big toe, which governs push-off.
  • The resolution of forefoot swelling, which governs what running shoes fit.
  • Your starting point: getting back to a 5k is not the same as getting back to marathon training.

Endoscopic TightRope® correction, by not cutting the bone, removes the wait for an osteotomy to heal; the other factors still apply.

The progression, in order

First, walking normally and without pain, over reasonable distances and in normal shoes. Until that is solid, there is no point considering what comes next.

Then low-impact exercise that rebuilds cardiovascular capacity without loading the forefoot: stationary bike, cross-trainer, swimming. This is the stage most often skipped and the one that protects most.

Next, specific strength and mobility work: the intrinsic foot muscles, the calf complex, and restoring extension of the big toe. Physiotherapy contributes a lot here.

Finally, graded running, starting with short jogging intervals alternated with walking on a soft surface, building volume before intensity and leaving rest days between sessions.

Signs to ease back

  • Forefoot pain during a run, or appearing the following day.
  • Swelling that returns or increases after sessions.
  • Limping, or changing your footstrike to avoid an area.
  • Sharp, localised pain over one metatarsal, which should be reviewed without delay.

Dropping back a step in the progression and consolidating it is always quicker than treating an overload injury.

Footwear matters more than it did

An operated foot tolerates the wrong shoe less well. Look for a running shoe with a genuinely wide forefoot, good cushioning, and a sole that does not force excessive extension of the big toe at push-off.

If you used orthotics before surgery, they need reviewing: the mechanics of the foot have changed and the old ones may no longer be appropriate.

Frequently asked questions

Will I be able to run as I did before?

Returning to an active life is one of the goals of treatment and most patients achieve it. The timeline depends on the procedure performed, on your recovery and on your starting point.

Can I run while there is still some swelling?

Residual swelling at the end of the day is normal for months and does not by itself prevent progression. What is a warning sign is swelling that increases after running.

What if I only want to get back to long walks?

The progression is the same but shorter. Walking long distances asks less of the foot than running, though the volume should still be built up in stages.
