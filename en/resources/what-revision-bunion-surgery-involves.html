What revision bunion surgery involves

A second operation is not a repeat of the first. It is a different problem, with a different work-up and expectations worth setting from the outset.

Last reviewed: August 2026

When revision is considered

The usual reasons are persistent pain that does not settle with time, recurrence of the deformity, stiffness of the first joint, residual malalignment, an osteotomy that failed to unite, irritation from fixation hardware, and transfer metatarsalgia appearing after the first operation.

Patients also present with a result that may be technically acceptable but has not solved their problem. That dissatisfaction is a legitimate reason for assessment.

Why it is more complex than a first operation

  • The anatomy is altered: the usual surgical planes may be obscured by scar tissue.
  • There may be loss of length or of bone stock after the previous procedure.
  • Existing hardware shapes the approach and sometimes has to be removed.
  • Soft tissues are less elastic and tolerate correction less well.
  • Healing is less predictable than in a foot that has not been operated on.

The work-up

A well-planned revision starts with understanding what happened. Examination assesses the real alignment, movement at the first joint, stability of the first ray and the exact points of pain.

Weight-bearing X-rays are essential, because they show the angles with the foot under load. Depending on the case, CT is added — particularly useful where non-union is suspected or to assess the rotational component — or MRI where soft-tissue involvement is suspected.

Bringing the reports and images from the previous surgery is genuinely useful. Knowing which technique was used saves a lot of guesswork.

What the procedure may involve

It depends entirely on what failed. It may mean correcting the residual deviation of the metatarsal, removing or exchanging hardware, treating an osteotomy that did not unite, releasing and rebalancing contracted soft tissues, adding bone graft where length has been lost, or rebalancing the whole forefoot where there is transfer metatarsalgia.

Guided by the SPARE™ Method, the approach is still to preserve everything healthy and limit the intervention to what is necessary. In an already-operated foot that principle matters more, not less, because the resources available are more limited.

Realistic expectations

It is worth stating plainly: the goal of a revision is usually a functional, pain-free foot rather than a foot identical to one never operated on. Movement may not fully return, and recovery tends to be longer than after a first operation.

That said, the improvement in pain and function is usually substantial, and for most patients who reach this point it is exactly what they need.

Frequently asked questions

What if I have had bunion surgery before?

Previous bunion surgery does not necessarily rule out further treatment. Dr. D’Angelo has extensive experience in revision surgery, treating recurrent deformity, persistent pain, stiffness, hardware problems and unsatisfactory results.

How long should there be between the two operations?

It depends on the reason. A hardware problem can be dealt with sooner than a recurrence, which needs the deformity to have stabilised and the tissues to have matured.

Is it more painful than the first?

Not necessarily, but recovery tends to be somewhat slower because the tissues have been operated on before and heal less predictably.
