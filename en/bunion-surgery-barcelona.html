Bunion surgery (hallux valgus) in Barcelona

The bunion is the most common forefoot deformity and the reason most patients come to this practice. Dr. Fabio D’Angelo corrects it using endoscopic and minimally invasive techniques that preserve healthy anatomy wherever it is clinically appropriate.

What is a bunion?

A bunion, or hallux valgus, is a progressive deformity in which the big toe drifts towards the smaller toes while the first metatarsal moves the other way. The result is the characteristic bony prominence at the base of the toe, on the inner border of the foot.

It is not simply a lump: it is a change in the alignment of the entire first column of the foot. That is why bunions tend to worsen over time and, left untreated, can cause pain, inflammation, difficulty finding comfortable shoes, altered gait mechanics and knock-on problems across the rest of the forefoot.

Bunion symptoms

Symptoms vary widely from person to person, and the size of the deformity does not reliably predict how much pain it causes. The most common are:

  • A painful prominence at the base of the big toe, worse in closed shoes.
  • Redness, swelling or bursitis over the prominence.
  • Progressive drift of the big toe towards the second toe, sometimes overlapping it.
  • Pain under the ball of the foot from transferred load (metatarsalgia).
  • Calluses and hard skin at pressure points.
  • Stiffness or loss of movement in the joint.
  • Increasing difficulty finding shoes that do not rub.

Causes and risk factors

Bunions have a strong hereditary component: what is inherited is not the bunion itself but the foot type and biomechanical pattern that allow it to develop. Other factors act on that predisposition:

  • Family history and inherited foot shape.
  • Ligamentous laxity and instability of the first metatarsophalangeal joint.
  • Flat foot or excessive pronation, which increases load on the first ray.
  • Narrow, pointed or high-heeled shoes, which accelerate a deformity already present.
  • Inflammatory arthritis, such as rheumatoid arthritis.
  • Hormonal and connective-tissue changes, more common in women.

Non-surgical treatment: what is tried first

Surgery is not the first step. Conservative treatment does not correct the deformity — no exercise, insole or spacer returns the bone to its place — but it can control symptoms and relieve overload for a long time. It usually involves moving to wide, flexible lasts, custom orthotics to redistribute pressure, physiotherapy and intrinsic muscle work, and occasional anti-inflammatories during flare-ups.

When these measures stop being enough and pain starts limiting daily life, it is time to consider surgical correction.

When is surgery indicated?

The decision to operate rests on your symptoms and your quality of life, not on how the foot looks or how large the deformity is. Surgery is considered when there is persistent pain limiting daily activities, difficulty walking or exercising, a progressing deformity, an inability to wear normal shoes, or when associated problems have appeared such as bursitis, hammer toes or pain under the forefoot.

Dissatisfaction with a previous operation is another common reason to consult: Dr. D’Angelo regularly handles revision bunion surgery for recurrent deformity, persistent pain or hardware-related problems.

Dr. D’Angelo’s technique

Traditional bunion surgery has historically relied on osteotomy: cutting and repositioning the bone to realign the toe. The SPARE™ Method — Surgery Preserving Anatomy, Restoring, Endoscopically — challenges that approach wherever it is clinically appropriate, preserving healthy anatomy and avoiding unnecessary bone cuts.

Its most advanced expression is endoscopic TightRope® bunion correction, of which Dr. D’Angelo is a pioneer. Instead of dividing the bone, alignment between the first and second metatarsals is restored with a high-strength suture system, worked endoscopically through very small incisions. His experience with the technique allows him to treat a wider spectrum of deformities than is usually considered suitable for this approach.

Where the deformity, bone quality or associated arthritis demand it, the plan incorporates complementary percutaneous or minimally invasive techniques. The aim does not change: the least intervention necessary to achieve a stable, lasting correction. The full range is set out under foot and ankle surgical procedures.

Recovery

Recovery depends on the severity of the bunion and the procedure performed, and Dr. D’Angelo will give you a personalised plan. Broadly, with minimally invasive and endoscopic techniques most patients can bear weight in a protective post-operative shoe within the first few days; swelling peaks over the first weeks and settles gradually across several months.

The return to normal footwear generally follows the resolution of swelling, and going back to work is quicker for desk-based roles than for jobs that involve long periods standing. Sport is resumed in stages: walking and low-impact exercise first, higher-impact activity later as healing progresses.

Frequently asked questions

Is bunion surgery painful?

You will not feel pain during the procedure, as appropriate anaesthesia is used. After surgery it is normal to have some discomfort and swelling, especially in the first few days. Most patients manage pain well with the prescribed medication, rest, elevation and local cold.

When can I walk after bunion surgery?

Walking is encouraged in line with your personalised recovery plan. Many patients can bear weight in a protective post-operative shoe soon after surgery, though the timeline varies with the severity of the bunion and the procedure performed.

Can the bunion come back?

No surgical procedure can guarantee a bunion will never recur. However, careful planning, precise correction and following the post-operative instructions significantly reduce the risk. Dr. D’Angelo’s goal is a stable, durable correction that preserves the natural function of the foot.

Can both feet be operated on at the same time?

In selected cases, yes. Whether simultaneous surgery is appropriate depends on your general health, the severity of the deformities, the support you have at home and your recovery goals. This is assessed at the consultation.
