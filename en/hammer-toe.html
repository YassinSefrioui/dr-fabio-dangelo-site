Hammer toes: surgical correction in Barcelona

Hammer toes are deformities caused by an imbalance between the tendons that bend and straighten the toe. Treated early, while the deformity is still flexible, the options are far simpler.

What is a hammer toe?

A hammer toe develops when one or more toes become permanently bent through muscle and tendon imbalance. The middle joint of the toe flexes upwards into an angle, and the tip of the toe points down against the floor.

It is distinguished from related deformities by which joint is involved: in a claw toe both joints flex while the base of the toe extends, and in a mallet toe the deformity is confined to the joint at the tip. Treatment changes accordingly, which is why clinical examination is decisive.

The more important distinction in clinic is a different one: whether the deformity is flexible — the toe can still be straightened by hand — or rigid, once the joint has fixed in that position. A flexible deformity allows more conservative procedures.

Symptoms

  • One or more toes permanently bent.
  • Pain over the top of the toe from rubbing against footwear.
  • Calluses over the flexed joint and at the tip of the toe.
  • Pain under the forefoot, at the base of the affected toe.
  • Progressive stiffness and loss of movement.
  • Difficulty finding shoes that do not compress the toes.

Causes and risk factors

  • Imbalance between the flexor and extensor tendons of the toe.
  • The presence of a bunion, which pushes against the second toe and is one of the most common causes.
  • Short or narrow-toed footwear worn over years.
  • Cavus foot, with a high arch and forefoot overload.
  • Neurological conditions or inflammatory arthritis.
  • Previous trauma to the toe.

Non-surgical treatment

While the deformity remains flexible it can be managed with footwear that has a deep, wide toe box and does not force the toe into flexion, silicone orthoses and spacers that hold the toe aligned, podiatric offloading of the calluses, orthotics if the forefoot is overloaded, and stretching and strengthening of the intrinsic foot muscles.

These measures relieve pain and rubbing but do not reverse the deformity. Once the toe becomes rigid, they stop being effective.

When is surgery indicated?

Surgery is considered when pain persists despite a change of footwear, when calluses or friction wounds keep recurring, when the deformity is progressing towards rigidity, or when it prevents normal shoes being worn.

It is very common to correct hammer toes in the same operation as the bunion that caused them. Treating the toe alone, without correcting what is pushing it, invites recurrence.

Dr. D’Angelo’s technique

Dr. Fabio D’Angelo corrects both flexible and rigid deformities, matching the procedure to the joint involved and the degree of stiffness. In flexible deformities, rebalancing the soft tissues and releasing the contracted structures is often enough; in rigid ones the joint itself must also be addressed to restore alignment.

Following the SPARE™ Method, the operation is planned percutaneously or minimally invasively wherever clinically appropriate, through very small incisions and preserving as much healthy anatomy as possible. The aim is to relieve pain, remove pressure points and calluses, restore alignment and improve how the foot fits into a shoe. More detail under foot and ankle surgical procedures.

Recovery

Hammer toe correction usually allows early weight-bearing in a post-operative shoe, according to the plan you are given. It is normal for the toe to remain swollen and somewhat stiff for weeks, and in some cases strapping or splinting of the toe is prescribed through the initial healing period.

Where the correction is carried out alongside a bunion, recovery follows the timeline of the primary procedure.

Frequently asked questions

Can a hammer toe be straightened without surgery?

Orthoses, spacers and appropriate footwear relieve pain and rubbing, and can slow progression while the deformity is flexible, but they do not reverse it. Once the toe has become rigid, a stable correction requires surgery.

Why have I developed hammer toes when my problem is a bunion?

They are related problems. As the big toe drifts, it pushes against the second toe and alters load distribution across the forefoot, which encourages deformity in the neighbouring toes. That is why both are often corrected in the same operation.

Will the toe be stiff after the operation?

Some loss of movement is expected, particularly where the deformity was already rigid before surgery. The priority is a toe that is aligned, pain-free and no longer rubbing against footwear.
